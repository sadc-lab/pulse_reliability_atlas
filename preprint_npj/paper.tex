\abstract{Pulse is an actively maintained open-source adult-physiology simulator, yet its maintainers report no formal sensitivity analysis of its operational inputs. We present the first open reliability atlas across its critical-care envelope: Morris elementary-effects sampling of seven inputs, four severity scenarios, male and female bodies (1{,}280 simulations). Sensitivity is skewed: three inputs carry a median 93~\% of each output's total. A four-flag classifier reveals failure modes: two operator-actionable, one sex-stratified. Tidal-volume instability (20.6~\% of runs) is consistent with patient--ventilator dyssynchrony; locking the VC-AC trigger to $-2$~cmH$_2$O collapses that variability. At FiO$_2$~=~1.0, transient hyperoxia makes 120-second runs underestimate plateau PaO$_2$; 300-second healthy-lung reruns give a median 643~mmHg. Solver divergence was all-male in an input-extreme regime (20/1{,}280); reducing the male blood-volume baseline to the 4{,}082~mL floor eliminated it. Runs and flags are released openly, showing users which regions need checking before training on Pulse output.}

\keywords{Pulse Physiology Engine, digital twin, synthetic patient data, Morris elementary effects, sensitivity analysis, uncertainty quantification, ARDS, mechanical ventilation, VC-AC ventilation, critical care, machine learning training data, operational envelope, reproducibility}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Longitudinal health data of the kind needed to represent a patient's evolving state are often scarce and incomplete~\cite{katsoulakis2024digitaltwins}. Digital twins for health (virtual, often mechanistic models of human physiology used for in-silico trials, medical-device design, and synthetic-patient generation) are proliferating across healthcare~\cite{katsoulakis2024digitaltwins}. Synthetic data can help address data scarcity, account for under-represented groups and simulate unseen scenarios~\cite{vanbreugel2024synthetic}. Data from a physiological simulator, including infrequent adverse conditions that real-world datasets often under-represent, have been used to pre-train a glucose-forecasting and hypoglycemia-detection model subsequently evaluated on real-world datasets~\cite{hwang2025sim2real}. Outside medicine, a recent technical report on a large multimodal model attributes most of the gains observed in its post-training, under a fixed optimisation procedure, to the scale, diversity and verifiability of its synthesised training data and environments~\cite{deepseek2026v41flash}. In biomedicine, a perspective on synthetic data argues that their value will likely depend less on generation alone than on careful integration with real-world data, layered validation and digital-twin systems~\cite{victoriano2026sim2real}.

Mechanistic simulators supply physiological time series from explicitly specified inputs, with model-internal states accessible by construction. Mechanistic origin does not, however, make their outputs automatically reliable. The Pulse Physiology Engine (Kitware, Apache-2.0) is one such actively maintained open-source whole-body simulator~\cite{bray2019}. It models each physiological system as a small network of coupled compartments rather than full three-dimensional anatomy (a lumped-parameter approach), delivering real-time cardiovascular, respiratory, gas-exchange, and pharmacological simulation (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s1}). Pulse has been applied to (1)~ventilator-safety what-if analysis~\cite{webb2020}, (2)~validation of its own mechanical-ventilator model against a physical reference simulator~\cite{webb2022vent}, (3)~medical education and simulation training on virtual-patient and emergency-medicine platforms~\cite{pulseinaction}, and (4)~general-purpose computational physiology~\cite{bray2019}. Its respiratory dynamics have also been used independently as a source of simulated data for learning and benchmarking ventilator-control policies in Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)~\cite{ward2024ventilator}. A review of synthetic data in biomedicine cites Pulse as an example of a mechanistic generator~\cite{vanbreugel2024synthetic}.

Yet, to our knowledge, no public map of how the engine responds across its user-facing \emph{input} envelope has been published. Most of that work probes Pulse's \emph{outputs}, validating them against reference data, or, in one external study, falsifying intervention--outcome behaviour on a real cohort. Webb et al.'s ventilator-sharing study~\cite{webb2020} does sweep inputs (12{,}642 unique patient and ventilator combinations spanning respiratory compliance, diffusion impairment and positive end-expiratory pressure) and rank parameters by outcome correlation. That work, however, is over the \emph{mismatch} between two equal-sized 70~kg bodies sharing one ventilator, scored against a single composite safety outcome under pressure-mode ventilation with respiratory rate, I:E ratio and respiratory resistance held fixed. It resolves neither each input's influence on each physiological output, nor input clamping, nor solver divergence.

The maintainers state on the project's public FAQ that ``we have not performed a systematic forward propagation or inverse quantification of model uncertainty, nor have we conducted a formal sensitivity analysis''~\cite{pulsefaq}. The upstream repository does carry a single 2020 sensitivity study in its source tree, tagged \texttt{STUDY\_SENSITIVITY\_ANALYSIS\_1\_0\_0}. That study targets internal lumped-parameter circuit values for model calibration --- cardiovascular and respiratory resistances and compliances, which cannot be measured clinically. These are a different parameter class from the user-facing operational inputs that downstream applications specify. A prior external assessment of Pulse on a clinical dataset, by Cornish et al.~\cite{cornish2023}, applied a causal-falsification framework to MIMIC-III sepsis trajectories and reported intervention-response errors concentrated on electrolyte, metabolic, acid--base and dermal outputs, largely outside the present atlas's six-output cardio-respiratory panel. Without such a shared public map, downstream users may need to establish a use-case-specific working envelope independently.

We centre this work on the ARDS severity axis for two reasons. First, Pulse models mechanical ventilation of an impaired lung, a regime whose downstream reliability remains incompletely characterised. Second, mapping the adult engine's sensitivity and failure modes is the prerequisite for adapting it to pediatric ARDS, where we ultimately want simulated cohorts to support machine-learning prediction of ventilation-dependent blood gases in critically ill children. An adaptation cannot be assessed against an unmapped adult substrate.

The objective of this study was therefore to build the first open reliability atlas across Pulse's adult critical-care operational envelope, organised around three questions. \emph{How} strongly does each input drive each output, and where does that influence stop being linear --- because the response bends, or because inputs interact? \emph{Does} the engine run a submitted setting as given, or silently clamp it? And \emph{does} the solver diverge or never reach steady state?

We varied seven user-facing operational inputs across their accepted ranges: heart rate (HR), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP, DBP), respiratory rate (RR), tidal volume (Vt), inspired oxygen fraction (FiO$_2$) and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP). We used a Morris elementary-effects design~\cite{morris1991,campolongo2007}, a global screening method that varies one input at a time along randomised trajectories; we call the resulting batch of runs the \emph{sweep}. Six steady-state outputs were tracked: HR, mean arterial pressure (MAP), delivered tidal volume, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO$_2$), and arterial oxygen and carbon dioxide partial pressures (PaO$_2$, PaCO$_2$). Four clinical scenarios (a healthy baseline and three ARDS severities) crossed with two adult reference patient bodies, male and female, gave 1{,}280 simulations. A four-flag steady-state classifier then labelled every run: the setup input was refused, the solver diverged, a steady-state output fell outside hard physiological bounds, or the run never settled within the simulation window. Failures are categorised rather than discarded, so the atlas stays complete. We provide a reusable run-level classifier and a per-cell map that sets review priority. Neither is an external physiological validation, and the runs that pass screening are not a representative subset. The core sampling design, classifier thresholds and primary analysis plan were preregistered, and deviations are reported explicitly. The pipeline is reproducible from a single Docker image with a fixed seed and released open-source under Apache-2.0.

\subsection{Overview of the reliability atlas}\label{sec:results-overview}

The atlas comprises 1{,}280 simulations spanning eight cells of 160 runs each, one cell per clinical scenario (S1 healthy, S2 mild, S3 moderate, S4 severe ARDS) and patient sex (Figure~\ref{fig:design}). Every run carries the four preregistered flags --- \texttt{clamped}, \texttt{diverged}, \texttt{implausible} and \texttt{not\_steady\_state} --- defined with their trigger conditions in Table~\ref{tab:flag_defs}. Methods~\S\ref{sec:methods-detector} lists the hard physiological bounds behind \texttt{implausible}.

Of the four preregistered hypotheses (H1--H4, defined in Methods~\S\ref{sec:methods-stats}), H3 and H4 are settled by Table~\ref{tab:flag_summary}. H3 is supported: solver divergence appears outside the previously characterised ARDS instability band, on 20 / 1{,}280 runs whose sex stratification is the subject of \S\ref{sec:results-sex}. H4 is supported: its preregistered criterion is a sex difference in clamping, divergence or $\mu^{*}$ ranking, and that divergence asymmetry alone satisfies it. For H2, silent input clamping, the preregistered \texttt{clamped} category remained empty at setup (0 / 1{,}280 across every cell); because that log-based flag cannot detect silent substitution, the input-instantiation audit below provides the complementary check, and together they leave H2 \emph{not supported}. H1 is settled in \S\ref{sec:results-morris}.

That audit, conducted post hoc, compared every submitted value with the value Pulse serialises after ingestion, and found exact agreement for all 10{,}160 input contracts that reached ingestion. A contract is one value submitted in one configuration block; the design holds 10{,}240 of them. The remaining 80 ventilator contracts belong to 20 runs that failed during patient stabilisation before the ventilator action was submitted; they enter neither numerator nor denominator. Both results are bounded by the sampled envelope and establish nothing about the clinically plausible adult-ICU range outside it. Pulse does make one explicit downstream adjustment. Where FiO$_2$~=~1.0 was requested and the ventilator action was reached, the ventilator model caps the inspired oxygen fraction at 0.9996, with a logged error on all 195 runs concerned. The ambient mixture already carries CO$_2$, so exactly 1.0 cannot be composed (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-clamp}).

Retention is strongly cell-dependent and depends on which screen is applied. Under the four-flag screen, 663 of 1{,}280 runs (51.8~\%) are clean, ranging 3.43-fold across cells, from 144 of 160 in moderate ARDS on the male body down to 42 of 160 in the healthy female cell. Under the hard-failure-free screen, which admits runs flagged only \texttt{not\_steady\_state}, 1{,}134 of 1{,}280 (88.6~\%) survive, a 1.51-fold spread; the two screens deplete opposite ends of the severity axis. The per-cell breakdown is in Table~\ref{tab:screening-retention}, and Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s4} decomposes the \texttt{not\_steady\_state} population that separates the two screens into its trigger sub-modes.

\subsection{Primary input-sensitivity rankings}\label{sec:results-morris}

Figure~\ref{fig:morris} shows the Morris $\mu^{*}$, the input-importance metric, for the six outputs across the eight cells and seven swept inputs. Preregistered hypothesis H1 is supported in 48 of 48 cell $\times$ output combinations: the top-three inputs account for 0.658--0.998 of the summed $\mu^{*}$, with a median top-3 fraction of 0.928. The lowest values, from 0.658 to 0.727, all fall in severe-ARDS gas-exchange cells, where sensitivity spreads across more inputs, consistent with the model's severity-dependent gas-exchange changes. Cardiovascular outputs are cardio-input dominated, output HR by input HR and MAP by SBP in every cell, while gas-exchange outputs are ventilator-driven: FiO$_2$ dominates PaO$_2$ in mild and moderate ARDS. In severe ARDS its $\mu^{*}$ on PaO$_2$ falls sharply; it is still rank 1 in S4\_M, on a single elementary-effect pair, and is not estimable in S4\_F under the four-flag screen.

Influence is not proportionality. Applying the preregistered $\sigma / \mu^{*} \geq 0.6$ threshold ($\sigma$ is the standard deviation of an input's elementary effects) to each cell's top-three inputs, and retaining only those that clear it in every draw under each of five independent seeds, leaves 23 of the 144, spread over 17 of the 48 combinations; all seven inputs and all four scenarios appear, so departure from a linear, additive response is common rather than localised. The ratio is the standard Morris proxy for non-linearity \emph{or} input--input interaction and does not separate the two, so what is mapped here is their combined footprint. Delivered tidal volume is the most affected output, in 8 of the 23, and is also the channel whose oscillation dominates the \texttt{not\_steady\_state} flags (\S\ref{sec:results-vcac}), so the two findings cannot appear on the same runs: in scenario S1 the delivered-Vt coefficient of variation exceeds the 0.05 threshold on 139 of 320 runs and none of those 8 falls there, against 36 of 320 runs and 3 of the 8 in S3. That map is therefore bounded by run survival --- the regime that destabilises delivered volume most is the one in which that instability is least measurable. The same mechanism sets how much data each index rests on: across the 144 top-three entries the median index is computed from 7 of the 20 possible elementary-effect pairs, 25 rest on three or fewer, and 9 rest on a single pair, five of those being their combination's rank-1 input. For those nine, $\sigma$ is zero by construction rather than by measurement, and no bootstrap interval is estimable. All nine come from just two inputs, each in a single cell: tidal volume in cell S1\_F and FiO$_2$ in cell S4\_M. Per-entry pair counts are released with the indices. The top-three input sets are cross-checked against a SHAP surrogate on the same runs in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s13}. They are also re-derived under the hard-failure-free screen in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s13-screen}, and H1 holds in all 48 combinations under both the four-flag and the hard-failure-free screens. On the 30 combinations whose candidate set (the inputs carrying at least one retained pair) is the same under both screens, the rank-1 input is preserved in all 30, while the identity of ranks two and three changes in 11. Under this screen comparison, rank 1 was therefore more stable than the ordering below it, and the full ordering is not resolved by this design.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{VC-AC tidal-volume instability and the $-2$~cmH$_2$O trigger lever}{VC-AC tidal-volume instability and the -2 cmH2O trigger lever}}\label{sec:results-vcac}

Across the full sweep, the single largest source of \texttt{not\_steady\_state} flags is tidal-volume (Vt) oscillation under volume-controlled assist-control (VC-AC) ventilation, quantified as the delivered Vt coefficient of variation over the final 60~s of the 120~s recording. Vt oscillation is the \emph{primary} such flag on 20.6~\% of runs: the delivered breath volume never settles to a single value but keeps changing from breath to breath, a genuinely oscillating output rather than a solver failure. The effect concentrates in healthy adults and falls markedly with increasing ARDS severity, strongest where the set respiratory rate is high and lung compliance is normal. This is consistent with \emph{patient--ventilator dyssynchrony}, well documented clinically in volume-control ventilation~\cite{figueroacasas2016}; the specific conditions that produce it here are our own in-silico finding.

A follow-up experiment isolated the mechanism. Re-running 64 of the affected configurations with Pulse's \texttt{InspirationPatientTriggerPressure} (the airway-pressure drop below PEEP at which a patient effort triggers a breath) set to $-2$~cmH$_2$O collapses the median Vt CV from 0.351 to 0.016, and 78.1~\% of the locked-trigger reruns drop below the 0.05 steady-state threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:corrections}a). The lock is an exploration lever for lower-variability Vt delivery, not a clinical recommendation: it replaces the engine's respiratory-model trigger with a fixed pressure threshold, loosening the coupling between breath delivery and the modelled respiratory drive that reproduces effort-dependent breath-to-breath variability in Pulse's default configuration. In a real patient the same shift would leave sub-threshold inspiratory efforts unrewarded, a form of patient--ventilator dyssynchrony~\cite{thille2006} that is clinically undesirable~\cite{blanch2015}. The full protocol, the two worst-affected configurations and the per-run values are in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s8}.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Transient hyperoxia regime at FiO$_2$~=~1.0}{Transient hyperoxia regime at FiO2 = 1.0}}\label{sec:results-hyperoxia}

A second cluster of preregistered classifier flags concentrates on the highest inspired-oxygen settings. Within the locked 120-second simulation window, Pulse's steady-state mean PaO$_2$ exceeds the classifier's upper bound of 600~mmHg on 34 of the 126 \texttt{implausible}-flagged runs, and 91~\% of these excursions occur at FiO$_2 \geq 0.95$. A 60-run rerun separated an engine defect from a window-length artefact. Each configuration was re-executed from time zero with the recorded duration raised from 120 to 300 seconds, and the plateau taken over the final 60 seconds ($t = 240$--$300$~s). The result supports the artefact: in the S1 (healthy) cohort, 40 of 44 reruns (90.9~\%) converged to a stable plateau under the preregistered drift criterion (Figure~\ref{fig:corrections}b). Three of those 40 plateau above 1{,}400~mmHg, beyond the sea-level barometric pressure of 760~mmHg and so physically impossible; they belong to a six-run solver-instability regime noted below, three of whose members are drift-converged and three not, so 37 of 44 (84.1~\%) converge below the supplement's wider 700~mmHg upper bound, which is distinct from the locked 600~mmHg bound above. The S1 plateau median over all 40 drift-converged runs was 643~mmHg, sitting approximately 20~mmHg \emph{below} the alveolar-gas-equation prediction~\cite{west2020respiratory} rather than above it, which rules out a cohort-wide oxygenation runaway. It does not establish that the plateau level itself is right, which would take validation against measured arterial data and is outside the scope of a curation map.

Hyperoxia studies on Pulse should therefore record at least 300 seconds of simulation before extracting a steady-state plateau. In the audited hyperoxia and Vt-oscillation runs, window-length sensitivity was concentrated in PaO$_2$ and delivered Vt, and threshold exceedances were uncommon for HR, MAP and PaCO$_2$ (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s16-window}). Extended-window runs on healthy lungs at FiO$_2$ = 1.0 can additionally enter a separate solver-instability regime, far more often on the female reference body than on the male, so users running them should screen each PaO$_2$ trajectory individually. Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s9} gives the full protocol, the time-to-plateau statistic and the instability characterisation.

\subsection{Sex-stratified solver divergence and blood-volume dependence}\label{sec:results-sex}

Across the full 1{,}280-run sweep, solver divergence is rare but strikingly sex-stratified. 20 runs (1.6~\%) failed to complete with finite output, and \emph{all 20 fell on the male reference body}, against zero divergences across the 640 runs on the female reference body (Figure~\ref{fig:sex_bv}a). The 20 divergence events are not spread uniformly through the input space: they span only 8 distinct operational-input vectors, and FiO$_2$ was requested at its 1.0 boundary in 13 of the 20, although every divergence preceded the ventilator action that would have applied it (\S\ref{sec:results-overview}). The asymmetry belongs to those input extremes rather than to the engine as a whole: in a midrange-input sweep across the instability band of \S\ref{sec:results-secondary}, both reference bodies diverge, with the female body dominating the lower part of the band and the male body its upper part (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s5-regimes}).

A follow-up experiment identified a blood-volume dependence. Pulse's \texttt{SetupPatient.cpp} computes blood-volume baseline from a sex-independent allometric relation on body weight, yielding 4{,}803~mL for the 67.3~kg male reference body and 3{,}996~mL for the 56.2~kg female body. The two bodies therefore share essentially the same blood volume per kilogram ($\approx$71~mL\,kg$^{-1}$, within standard clinical estimates~\cite{nadler1962}), so, within Pulse's blood-volume calculation, what separates the two bodies is absolute volume, driven by body mass, rather than a sex-specific blood-volume formula. Re-executing the 20 originally-diverged male inputs with an explicit \texttt{BloodVolumeBaseline} override took the divergence count from 20 / 20 at the default to 0 / 20 at both an intermediate 4{,}400~mL bracket and the engine's own setup floor of 4{,}082~mL (Figure~\ref{fig:sex_bv}b). Removing the divergence does not by itself make a configuration usable: 12 of the 20 are clean at both override levels while 8 retain a flag --- SpO$_2$ pinned at saturation, the tidal-volume oscillation of \S\ref{sec:results-vcac}, and the high-FiO$_2$ PaO$_2$ modes of \S\ref{sec:results-hyperoxia} --- all of them trigger modes the locked classifier already defines, not new ones (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s10}, with the full override protocol). Divergence is one axis of sex-stratified behaviour; the steady-state values separate far less. Across the 24 (scenario $\times$ output) comparisons, the Wasserstein distance between the male and female clean-run distributions exceeds its permutation null on six, of which five survive Benjamini--Hochberg correction (Methods~\S\ref{sec:methods-stats}); all five are gas-exchange channels --- PaCO$_2$ in S1--S3, with SpO$_2$ and PaO$_2$ in S4. Heart rate, MAP and delivered tidal volume separate no further than a random relabelling of the same pooled runs. The input-importance rankings move less still: male and female Morris rankings stay concordant in the two scenarios where the comparison is computable, with Spearman $\rho$ point estimates of 0.75--1.00 across the 12 (scenario, output) cells of S2 and S3, only the mild-ARDS delivered-tidal-volume cell having a confidence interval that includes zero (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s6-rho}). Across the two reference bodies, where the solver fails therefore differs far more than which inputs dominate.

One caveat governs how any cross-body contrast should be read. The comparison is made on clean runs, and how many runs stay clean itself differs by sex --- 144 of 160 against 122 of 160 in S3, for instance --- so the two distributions are drawn from different subsets of the input grid, and a surviving distance cannot separate an effect on the physiology from an effect on which configurations survive the flag filter. Gas-exchange contrasts add a systematic ventilation-dose difference: at every matched absolute tidal-volume setting, the female reference body receives 19.8~\% more tidal volume per kilogram of actual body weight and 36.1~\% more per kilogram of ideal body weight, computed using the sex-specific Devine relation that Pulse applies at patient setup (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s6-dose}). Switching Pulse's sex flag also switches body composition, basal metabolic rate, haemoglobin, cardiovascular sizing and, through ideal body weight, anatomic dead space at once (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s10-breadth}), so no cross-body distance can be attributed to any single physiological pathway. None of this weakens the divergence finding above, which is an absolute-blood-volume effect demonstrated by direct intervention rather than inferred from a cross-body contrast.

\subsection{Secondary observations}\label{sec:results-secondary}

Four further observations bear on downstream interpretation: Pulse's ARDS impairment-fraction solver-instability band, a compensatory tachycardia that lifts mean output heart rate above the sampled input range in moderate and severe ARDS while input heart rate remains its dominant within-scenario determinant, a preregistered Sobol bolt-on (which apportions output variance among individual inputs and their interactions) that was triggered but not executed because the triggered design exceeded its locked compute budget, and a limitation of the flatline rule itself. All four are stated in full in the Supplementary Information and indexed in \S\,\ref{sec:s17-summary}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This work delivers the first open reliability atlas for the Pulse Physiology Engine across several adult critical-care operational scenarios, with its core design and primary analysis plan preregistered. Two operator-actionable corrections and a sex-stratified reliability axis emerge from the 1{,}280-run sweep. The first is a single configuration setting that markedly stabilises VC-AC tidal-volume delivery for synthetic-cohort generation. It does so at the cost of suppressing much of the effort-dependent variability Pulse models, so it is a data-generation tool rather than a clinical ventilation recommendation (\S\ref{sec:results-vcac}). The second is a steady-state-window correction for hyperoxia simulation at FiO$_2 = 1.0$ (\S\ref{sec:results-hyperoxia}). On a separate axis, the sweep reveals a previously uncharacterised sex-stratified solver-divergence cluster with an identified blood-volume dependence (\S\ref{sec:results-sex}). The per-cell traffic-light curation map (Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_light}) is the principal deliverable for downstream users. Its criteria carry a material post hoc deviation from the preregistered wording at the yellow--red boundary, declared with its sensitivity analysis in \S\ref{sec:methods-traffic}. The map is a curation and review-priority instrument, not a general physiological validation of the engine. Credibility frameworks in biomedical modelling treat sensitivity analysis and a bounded domain of applicability as components of assessment, while validation itself requires comparison against measured data~\cite{viceconti2021insilico}, which this work does not perform. A five-stage validation scheme has been proposed for synthetic biomedical data, running from internal plausibility through downstream utility, out-of-distribution generalisation and simulation-to-reality transfer to biological or clinical coupling~\cite{victoriano2026sim2real}. The atlas's run-level checks address only part of the first stage: they test whether runs are internally plausible, not whether they match real-world distributions.

The four-flag classifier can also be used to screen Pulse-generated synthetic patient cohorts before they are used to train machine-learning models. Its role is comparable to the verification and filtering stages of the synthetic-data pipeline in the technical report cited in the Introduction, where each synthesised agent task (one the model performs by acting in a software environment) carries its own verification system~\cite{deepseek2026v41flash}, with one difference: the classifier checks the simulator's behaviour, not agreement with patients. This use rests on the null result of \S\ref{sec:results-overview}, bounded to the sampled envelope: within Pulse's accepted setup envelope, no submitted input was silently altered at ingestion. This concerns setup-time input handling only; it does not imply that physiological states stay fixed once the simulation runs, nor establish Pulse's behaviour outside the sampled envelope. The traffic-light map in Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_light} sets cell-level review priority; run-level four-flag screening remains necessary irrespective of cell colour, because a tile's colour does not count runs flagged \texttt{not\_steady\_state}, and every use remains subject to use-case-specific validation. The green set is the same under both non-degenerate readings of the rule, and green does not establish physiological validity. The same map has a second audience: Pulse is embedded in named training products that present healthcare learners with real-time physiological and pharmacological data~\cite{pulseinaction}. An instructor selecting critical-care teaching scenarios can use the per-cell map to avoid regimes where the engine diverges or never settles.

Three findings call for care when Pulse-generated data are reused. The sex-stratified divergence cluster (\S\ref{sec:results-sex}) means that pooling male and female runs from the input-extreme regime without flag-stratified filtering ingests a spurious sex covariate. The compensatory tachycardia (\S\ref{sec:results-secondary}) means that input and output heart rates are not interchangeable in ARDS scenarios, even though input heart rate still drives output heart rate within each scenario. A model trained on Pulse cohorts would otherwise learn a compensatory response as a steady-state feature rather than as an active response to hypoxemia. The third survives flag-based curation altogether: sex-conditioned distributional differences remain detectable even among clean runs (\S\ref{sec:results-sex}), without a single mechanism or a scenario-independent direction of effect.

For the computational-physiology community, the atlas matters in two ways. First, it directly addresses the Pulse maintainers' own statement that no formal sensitivity or uncertainty quantification has been performed on user-facing operational inputs~\cite{pulsefaq}. The Morris screen finds sensitivity concentrated in a few inputs on every cell $\times$ output combination tested (\S\ref{sec:results-morris}). Second, the method carries over to other engines. It pairs a Morris screen of the operational inputs with a four-flag classifier that sorts every run into a category instead of discarding any. To our knowledge no comparable publicly available reliability atlas combining Morris screening with explicit clamping analysis has been published for either BioGears~\cite{baird2020biogears} or HumMod~\cite{hester2011hummod}. The same recipe could be applied to them by wrapping each engine's own inputs and outputs.

Prior external falsification work on MIMIC-III is complementary rather than overlapping: Cornish et al.~\cite{cornish2023} applied a causal-falsification framework to Pulse on MIMIC-III sepsis trajectories and reported 85 total Hoeffding-inequality rejections. Of these, 82 of 85 fall outside the four-channel overlap with this atlas (HR, MAP, PaO$_2$, PaCO$_2$): 80 on channels the present atlas does not assess, plus 2 on partial-overlap pressure channels. Only 3 of 85 fall inside that overlap. Cornish et al.\ test Pulse's chemistry and thermoregulation outputs against a real patient cohort undergoing clinical interventions; the present atlas tests its cardio-respiratory response to operational inputs. Neither maps how operational inputs drive the electrolyte channels Cornish's test flags most often, so a metabolic-and-renal reliability atlas is the natural next step.

Beyond guidance for downstream users, the atlas also serves the engine's maintainers: it provides, to our knowledge, the first systematic characterisation of an ARDS impairment-fraction solver-instability band ($[0.879, 0.946]$). It also offers further engine-level observations that target tooling, documentation and validation infrastructure rather than Pulse's underlying physiology (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s11}).

Six scope limits bound the conclusions; each is set out with its evidence in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s2-scope}. (i)~The atlas pins to Pulse v4.3.1; results apply identically to the build-only v4.3.2. (ii)~Patient anatomy is two adult reference bodies, so mass and height variation within sex is uncharacterised. (iii)~The envelope covers adult VC-AC ventilation only; pediatric translation, pressure-targeted modes and transient within-run dynamics are out of scope by design. (iv)~The Morris estimator discards any input pair containing a flagged run, which eliminates every clean FiO$_2$ pair in both healthy cells (S1\_M, S1\_F) and the severe-ARDS female cell (S4\_F). Those FiO$_2$ entries of Figure~\ref{fig:morris} are therefore blank, and the cross-sex Spearman comparison is confined to S2 and S3. (v)~The hard physiological bounds judge whether a \emph{sustained} steady-state mean is plausible, not whether a value is instantaneously attainable; they are a screening standard, not a claim that exceptional clinical trajectories are impossible. (vi)~Pulse enforces DBP/SBP $\leq$ 0.75, and configuration-time clipping shortened 9 of 20 DBP steps, so some SBP and DBP effects are not strict fixed-step Morris estimates. Two further caveats are of evidence rather than scope: no trajectory-count convergence analysis was performed, and shunt fraction was not recorded, so shunt-mediated interpretations throughout this work remain physiologically motivated rather than directly tested.

This atlas is meant as a baseline for further work. The four-flag classifier and the per-cell traffic-light are reusable without re-running the present sweep: the classifier for new runs recorded with the atlas's protocol, the map for configurations inside its eight cells. By characterising the adult operational envelope first, the atlas provides the methodological basis a pediatric translation layer would need. Whether these adult-model reliability patterns transfer to pediatric physiology remains open. Validating the cell-level classifications against real-world clinical databases such as MIMIC-IV would test them against real patient care. Simulated and real-world patient cohorts are complementary rather than interchangeable: mechanistic simulation provides controlled inputs and directly accessible model states, whereas real-world patient data retain the messiness of clinical practice. Whether a model trained on simulated cohorts performs on real-world patient data is a further question. One sim-to-real study addressed it by quantifying the distributional mismatch between its simulated training data and real-world datasets and by evaluating its model on the latter~\cite{hwang2025sim2real}; the atlas does neither. Models trained only on simulated data and tested on data from real patients, in ventilator-asynchrony detection and in aortic pressure estimation, performed reasonably, though below models trained on such data~\cite{bakkes2023asynchrony,bikia2020aortic}.

Pulse-generated cohorts are already used as machine-learning training data~\cite{ward2024ventilator}, and ventilation automation is already reaching the bedside: a recent multicentre randomised trial of closed-loop ventilation improved overall ventilation quality yet showed no difference in patient-centred outcomes~\cite{sinnige2026active}. Characterising a simulator's operational limits is therefore a precondition for using it to train models that may inform such care.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Pulse engine and configuration}\label{sec:methods-pulse}

All simulations used Pulse v4.3.1 (Kitware, Apache-2.0)~\cite{bray2019}, a real-time-capable lumped-parameter whole-body physiology engine whose respiratory sub-model is a 5-compartment lung~\cite{pulserespmethod}. The engine ran inside a single \texttt{pulse-atlas-engine} Docker image, pinned for the 1{,}280-run sweep so that every simulation runs against bit-identical engine binaries. Runs were orchestrated by a thin Python layer around Pulse's command-line interface, which writes one JSON configuration per Morris input vector --- reference body, ARDS scenario condition, ventilator action set, and the seven operational inputs at their sampled values --- and spawns a container from that image.

Re-running the pipeline reproduces the canonical outputs bit-for-bit at three successive stages, each checked against its own committed artefact: the input sample, the per-cell aggregate tables, and the derived statistics. Mechanical ventilation was restricted to volume-controlled assist-control (VC-AC) mode so that all runs remain directly comparable; pressure-targeted modes lie outside the atlas's scope. The dispatch hardware and the full environment are given in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s12-env}.

\subsection{Patient reference bodies}\label{sec:methods-patients}

Two adult reference bodies were used: a male body (age 18, 67.3~kg, 180~cm) and a female body (age 18, 56.2~kg, 163~cm). Both weights are U.S.\ Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) growth-chart 50th-percentile weight-for-age values for each sex~\cite{kuczmarski2002cdc}, taken from the CDC row at \texttt{Agemos}~=~216.5. The female height matches the CDC 50th percentile and Pulse's own standard female height; the male height is researcher-fixed. Supplement~\S\ref{sec:s3-bodies} carries the full provenance.

Pulse also refuses some setup values outright. Two of those constraints act on inputs swept here, a gate on systolic and diastolic blood pressure and acceptance bands on the initial heart and respiratory rates; both were verified against the Pulse source and by boundary-probe runs before the sweep (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s2}), and the sampler ranges were set inside them. A third, Pulse's limit on the pulse-pressure ratio, is deliberately not imposed on the Morris sample, because that would distort the sample geometry the elementary-effect estimator depends on; it is applied one step later, when each configuration is written, and every run records the requested value, the applied value and a clip flag (\S\ref{sec:methods-sweep}). Every configuration actually submitted to Pulse therefore lies inside the accepted envelope. A fourth constraint, a hard per-patient floor on the male body's blood-volume baseline, is never exercised by the sweep and is characterised separately in \S\ref{sec:results-sex}.

\subsection{Sweep design}\label{sec:methods-sweep}

Seven user-facing operational inputs were swept across their verified Pulse acceptance ranges: heart rate (HR, 50--110~bpm), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP 90--120~mmHg, DBP 60--80~mmHg with the post-clip pulse-pressure constraint), respiratory rate (RR, 8--20~breaths\,min$^{-1}$), tidal volume (Vt, 300--800~mL), inspired oxygen fraction (FiO$_2$, 0.21--1.0) and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP, 0--20~cmH$_2$O). Each range is anchored to a primary verification source, table by table, in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s2}. Mean arterial pressure was derived as $\mathrm{DBP} + (\mathrm{SBP} - \mathrm{DBP})/3$ rather than swept independently. The swept heart-rate and blood-pressure values are setup interventions honoured at ingestion rather than setpoints maintained against the engine's own reflexes: Pulse re-baselines those fields to the stabilised state at the end of stabilisation, and no action used here rewrites them afterwards.

Four scenarios spanned the adult critical-care severity range: a healthy baseline (S1, ARDS impairment fraction = 0) and three ARDS severities (S2, mild, impairment = 0.26; S3, moderate, impairment = 0.60; S4, severe, impairment = 0.95). At a low-tidal-volume reference ventilation setting, these impairment fractions place each scenario in a Berlin P/F band for the male reference body, and both that calibration and its sweep-wide FiO$_2$ dependence are tabulated in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s5-berlin}. The impairment fractions are therefore anchored to a clinically recognisable state rather than chosen as arbitrary engine settings. The mild, moderate and severe labels name the band each scenario was calibrated to, not a diagnosis of each run: individual runs vary FiO$_2$ and PEEP across the sweep and need not meet that band's P/F and PEEP criteria. The three severities also bracket the 0.91--0.945 solver-instability zone documented at design time (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s5}); the atlas's own continuous-severity sweep subsequently widened that band to $[0.879, 0.946]$ (\S\ref{sec:results-secondary}). Pulse represents severity as a single per-lung scalar driving four hard-coded, adult-calibrated interpolation curves, mapped in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s5-mapping}.

Sensitivity was screened with the Morris elementary-effects design~\cite{morris1991, campolongo2007}, a global screening method whose cost is linear in the number of inputs. The sample was generated with SALib~\cite{iwanaga2022,herman2017salib} using 20 trajectories at 4 grid levels per input and random seed 42. Each trajectory contributes $7 + 1 = 8$ model evaluations, giving 160 runs per (scenario $\times$ sex) cell and 1{,}280 runs across the full sweep. The trajectory count was fixed at 20 in preregistration as a computationally tractable design; it was not selected through a trajectory-count convergence analysis. Each run was recorded for 120 seconds of simulated time after Pulse's patient stabilisation and a 120-second ventilator-settling phase, with output channels sampled at 5-second intervals (13 samples in the final 60-second steady-state window).

\subsection{Steady-state classifier}\label{sec:methods-detector}

Every simulation was classified by a four-flag steady-state classifier before any statistical analysis. \texttt{clamped} is the preregistered label for a run whose Pulse setup log matches any of 5 documented input-rejection patterns --- 4 of them in force when the sweep ran, the fifth added afterwards to close a false negative on Pulse's lower-bound rejection format. Replaying both pattern sets over all 3{,}348 recorded setup logs reclassifies no run of this sweep. This log-based detector identifies \emph{explicit} setup rejection and cannot by itself detect a silent substitution; a post hoc input-instantiation audit closes that gap and is reported in \S\ref{sec:results-overview}. \texttt{diverged} fires when the simulation failed to complete with finite output, the CSV is missing, or the engine exited with a non-zero status. The category is wider than solver divergence by construction, since a setup rejection also exits non-zero, but in this sweep it holds nothing else: each of the 20 diverged runs carries Pulse's own \texttt{Could not converge to provided Convergence} declaration in its setup log, and none of the 1{,}260 completed runs does (\texttt{results/divergence\_timeline.csv}).

\texttt{implausible} fires when any steady-state output mean exceeds the locked hard physiological bounds: HR [20, 220]~bpm, MAP [30, 180]~mmHg, Vt [50, 1{,}500]~mL, SpO$_2$ [50, 100]~\%, PaO$_2$ [20, 600]~mmHg, PaCO$_2$ [10, 120]~mmHg. Unlike the swept inputs, the six tracked outputs have no acceptance envelope in Pulse: the engine reports whatever its solver produces and never rejects an output value. These bounds are therefore ours, a plausibility standard applied after the run rather than a constraint the engine enforces, and each is anchored to primary critical-care and respiratory-physiology literature in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-hard-bounds}.

\texttt{not\_steady\_state} fires when, over the final 60-second window, any tracked output flatlines, drifts beyond its per-channel clinical threshold, or varies beyond its coefficient-of-variation threshold. A flatline is variability below 1~\% of the mean \emph{and} a mean within 0.1~\% of a hard bound --- $\geq$~99.9~\% for the SpO$_2$ ceiling --- a rule written to catch a solver that has pinned an output and stopped advancing; the drift thresholds are tabulated in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-drift}.

The four flags are independent presence counts, so one run can fire several. Analyses that need one category per run resolve it by a priority order fixed after the sweep, \texttt{clamped} $>$ \texttt{diverged} $>$ \texttt{implausible} $>$ \texttt{not\_steady\_state} $>$ \texttt{clean}, following the chronology of failure within a run. The [60, 120]~s window was preregistered before any sweep data was collected, and a 22-run pilot on the healthy-male cell verified five preregistered gating criteria, among them detector self-consistency and Pulse determinism.

\subsection{Traffic-light curation map}\label{sec:methods-traffic}

The per-cell curation map (Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_light}) reduces each (scenario, sex, output) cell to one green / yellow / red verdict, built from two locked ingredients: the cell's combined diverged-plus-implausible flag rate, and its deviation from a baseline. The baseline for each output is that output's mean over the clean runs of the healthy-male (S1, M) cell, and the $10~\%$ and $30~\%$ deviation bands were preregistered before any data were collected; the full colour criteria are stated in the legend to Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_light}. Scoring every cell against a single (S1, M) baseline keeps male and female cells on one comparable scale, at the cost of folding legitimate male--female differences into the female cells' deviation term; Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-traffic} reports the re-scoring check that verifies this choice does not drive the classification.

\textbf{Post hoc deviation from the preregistered traffic-light rule.} The four-flag run-level classifier and its thresholds were preregistered, but the traffic-light aggregation was not implemented exactly as written. The preregistration stated that a cell would be red when its deviation exceeded $30~\%$ or when ``any flag'' was raised; the implemented rule treats \texttt{diverged} and \texttt{implausible} as hard flags, assigns yellow whenever their combined rate exceeds zero, and reserves red for a combined hard-flag rate above $30~\%$. The repository establishes that this implementation was committed after preregistration but not whether it was defined before or after the sweep outputs were inspected, so we record the altered yellow--red boundary as a material deviation. Under the implemented rule the map contains 7 green / 29 yellow / 12 red combinations; under the literal reading it would contain 7 green / 3 yellow / 38 red, with the same seven green and the same twelve red. We retain the implemented rule as the primary display because it preserves a graded distinction without creating a single additional green verdict, and neither the classifier, the map nor the reported counts were changed in response to this finding. The preregistered reading is reported alongside them as a sensitivity analysis in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-tl-sensitivity}.

\subsection{Statistical analyses}\label{sec:methods-stats}

Morris $\mu^{*}$ and $\sigma$ were computed per (scenario, sex, input, output) from the SALib sample~\cite{iwanaga2022,herman2017salib}. Each elementary effect divides the change in the output by the size of the input step expressed on that input's swept range mapped to $[0,1]$, so a $\mu^{*}$ is an output change per full-range move of its input. Indices carry non-parametric 95~\% bootstrap confidence intervals from 1{,}000 trajectory-level resamples. Four hypotheses were preregistered before data collection: H1, the top-three inputs account for at least half of the summed $\mu^{*}$ for each output; H2, at least 5~\% of the Morris sample hits at least one silent clamp; H3, at least one solver-divergence zone exists outside the previously characterised 0.91--0.945 band within the seven-input adult envelope; H4, the male and female reference bodies differ in clamping, divergence or $\mu^{*}$ ranking on at least one output, with no direction prespecified. Hypothesis~1 was evaluated per cell $\times$ output on the point-estimate top-3 fraction of summed $\mu^{*}$. That sum runs over the inputs carrying at least one clean elementary-effect pair, so it covers six inputs rather than seven in the 18 combinations where FiO$_2$ carries none (Figure~\ref{fig:morris}); the fractions consequently do not share a common denominator across cells. Hypotheses 2, 3 and 4 are resolved in \S\ref{sec:results-overview}.

Sex differences in the steady-state distributions were measured by per-(scenario $\times$ output) Wasserstein distances (the minimum ``work'' needed to reshape one probability distribution into the other) between the male and female clean-run distributions, tested against a permutation null of 2{,}000 random relabellings at the two original group sizes. The resulting 24-test family was corrected by Benjamini--Hochberg control of the false-discovery rate (the expected fraction of false positives among the rejected tests) at $q = 0.05$; with 2{,}000 relabellings the smallest non-zero empirical tail proportion is 1/2{,}000, and two tests reaching zero are reported conservatively as $p < 0.001$. Cross-sex agreement of input-importance rankings was quantified by the Spearman rank correlation ($+1$ when the two orderings agree, $-1$ when they are reversed) between male and female $\mu^{*}$ vectors per (scenario, output).

A Sobol bolt-on was preregistered as a conditional escalation on any top-three input whose $\sigma / \mu^{*}$ ratio reaches 0.6. Applied as preregistered, the trigger fires on 72 of the 144 top-three inputs, spanning 43 of the 48 combinations, while the same preregistration fixed the bolt-on's compute envelope at 1{,}500 additional runs. Because every output of a cell comes from the same Pulse run, one Sobol sample serves all six, so the cost scales with the eight firing cells rather than with the 43 combinations: 6{,}144 runs under the preregistered $64 \times (2k + 2)$ formula, or 3{,}584 if only the first-order and total indices the protocol asks for are computed. The bolt-on was therefore infeasible within the budget its own protocol declared, exceeding it by a factor of 2.4 to 4.1 on these two readings, and up to 5.5 on the wider set in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-sobol}, and is reported rather than executed. Because the preregistered trigger rests on the point ratio alone, we add a post hoc sensitivity analysis in which trajectories are resampled 1{,}000 times per combination and $\sigma / \mu^{*}$ is recomputed jointly on each draw; combinations whose lower bound clears 0.6 in every draw, under each of five independent seeds, are characterised in \S\ref{sec:results-morris}, and the full decomposition is in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-sobol}.

The full sampler, sweep driver, four-flag classifier, statistical-analysis pipeline and audit ledger are released open-source. The sampling design, the input ranges, the four flags and their thresholds, and the analysis plan were preregistered before data collection; the audit ledger was developed afterwards, to re-derive the computed claims in this paper from the canonical output artefacts.

\subsection{Use of AI tools}\label{sec:methods-ai}

\noindent Large language models (LLMs) were used substantially throughout this work, under direct supervision by S.N., for drafting, editing, refactoring and debugging both the analysis-pipeline code and the manuscript prose, and for in-repository replications, primary-source audits and a condensation pass. During iterative review, a second LLM, in a separate session, was repeatedly prompted to critique the first model's outputs, a loop reported as an additional source of challenge on text and code. Responsibility rests with the authors. S.N.\ personally reviewed and verified all AI-assisted outputs, including editorial revisions, consistency checks and methodological interpretations; reviewed, tested and committed every AI-assisted code change; and authored and locked all preregistered thresholds, hypotheses, statistical-test choices and experimental decisions before the corresponding analyses were run. Quantitative claims are traceable, as applicable, to primary sources, the preregistration, or entries in the open audit ledger released alongside the code, whose commit history records the audit findings, their evidence and the resulting corrections.
