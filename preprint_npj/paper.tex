\abstract{Pulse is an actively maintained open-source adult-physiology simulator, yet its maintainers report no formal sensitivity analysis of its operational inputs. We present the first open reliability atlas across its critical-care envelope: Morris elementary-effects sampling of seven inputs, four severity scenarios, male and female bodies (1{,}280 simulations). Sensitivity is skewed: three inputs carry a median 93~\% of each output's total. A four-flag classifier reveals failure modes: two operator-actionable, one sex-stratified. Tidal-volume oscillation (20.6~\% of runs) is consistent with patient--ventilator dyssynchrony; locking the VC-AC trigger to $-2$~cmH$_2$O collapses that variability. At FiO$_2$~=~1.0, transient hyperoxia makes 120-second runs underestimate plateau PaO$_2$; 300-second healthy-lung reruns give a median 643~mmHg. Solver divergence was all-male in an input-extreme regime (20/1{,}280); reducing the male blood-volume baseline to the 4{,}082~mL floor eliminated it. Runs and flags are released openly, showing users which regions need checking before training on Pulse output.}

\keywords{Pulse Physiology Engine, digital twin, synthetic patient data, sensitivity analysis, Morris elementary effects, mechanical ventilation, ARDS, reproducibility}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Longitudinal health data that capture a patient's evolving state are often scarce and incomplete~\cite{katsoulakis2024digitaltwins}. Digital twins for health (virtual, often mechanistic models of human physiology used for in-silico trials, medical-device design, and synthetic-patient generation) are proliferating~\cite{katsoulakis2024digitaltwins}, and synthetic data can help address data scarcity, account for under-represented groups and simulate unseen scenarios~\cite{vanbreugel2024synthetic}. For example, simulator data that include infrequent adverse conditions have been used to pre-train a glucose-forecasting and hypoglycemia-detection model later evaluated on real-world datasets~\cite{hwang2025sim2real}. Outside medicine, a recent technical report on a large multimodal model attributes most of its post-training gains, under a fixed optimisation procedure, to the scale, diversity and verifiability of its synthesised training data and environments~\cite{deepseek2026v41flash}. In biomedicine, a perspective on synthetic data argues that ``the value of synthetic data will likely depend less on generation alone and more on careful integration with real-world data, layered validation and digital twin systems''~\cite{victoriano2026sim2real}.

Mechanistic simulators generate physiological time series from explicitly specified inputs, with model-internal states accessible by construction, but a mechanistic origin does not make their outputs automatically reliable. The Pulse Physiology Engine (Kitware, Apache-2.0) is an actively maintained open-source whole-body simulator~\cite{bray2019}. It represents each physiological system as a small network of coupled compartments (a lumped-parameter approach) and simulates cardiovascular, respiratory, gas-exchange and pharmacological dynamics in real time (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s1}). Pulse has been used for ventilator-safety what-if analysis~\cite{webb2020}, validation of its own ventilator model against a physical reference simulator~\cite{webb2022vent}, medical education and simulation training~\cite{pulseinaction}, general-purpose computational physiology~\cite{bray2019}, and as a source of simulated data for learning and benchmarking ventilator-control policies in Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS)~\cite{ward2024ventilator}. A review of synthetic data in biomedicine cites Pulse as an example of a mechanistic generator~\cite{vanbreugel2024synthetic}.

Yet, to our knowledge, no public map of how the engine responds across its user-facing \emph{input} envelope has been published. The maintainers state on the project's public FAQ that ``we have not performed a systematic forward propagation or inverse quantification of model uncertainty, nor have we conducted a formal sensitivity analysis''~\cite{pulsefaq}. The only sensitivity study in the upstream repository (2020, tagged \texttt{STUDY\_SENSITIVITY\_ANALYSIS\_1\_0\_0}) varies internal model parameters that cannot be measured clinically (cardiovascular and respiratory resistances and compliances) for calibration purposes, not operational inputs such as heart rate, blood pressure or ventilator settings. Other assessments probe the engine's \emph{outputs}, either by validating them against reference data~\cite{webb2022vent} or, in the study by Cornish et al.~\cite{cornish2023}, by causal falsification on MIMIC-III sepsis trajectories, which located intervention-response errors mainly in electrolyte, metabolic, acid--base and dermal outputs. Webb et al.~\cite{webb2020} did sweep inputs (12{,}642 patient and ventilator combinations spanning respiratory compliance, diffusion impairment and positive end-expiratory pressure) and rank parameters by outcome correlation, but to score the \emph{mismatch} between two equal-sized 70~kg bodies sharing one ventilator against a single composite safety outcome, under pressure-mode ventilation with respiratory rate, I:E ratio and respiratory resistance held fixed. That design resolves neither each input's influence on each physiological output, nor input clamping, nor solver divergence. Without a shared public map, downstream users may need to establish a use-case-specific working envelope independently.

We focus on the ARDS severity axis for two reasons. First, Pulse models mechanical ventilation of an impaired lung, a regime whose downstream reliability remains incompletely characterised. Second, mapping the adult engine's sensitivity and failure modes is a prerequisite for adapting it to pediatric ARDS, where we ultimately aim to use simulated cohorts to train machine-learning models that predict ventilation-dependent blood gases in critically ill children; an adaptation cannot be assessed against an unmapped adult substrate.

Our objective was therefore to build the first open reliability atlas of Pulse's adult critical-care operational envelope, around three questions: (1)~how strongly does each input drive each output, and where does that influence become non-linear or interactive; (2)~does the engine run a submitted setting as given, or silently clamp it; and (3)~where does the solver diverge or fail to reach steady state?

We varied seven user-facing operational inputs across their accepted ranges: heart rate (HR), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP, DBP), respiratory rate (RR), tidal volume (Vt), inspired oxygen fraction (FiO$_2$) and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), using a Morris elementary-effects design~\cite{morris1991,campolongo2007} (a global screening method that varies one input at a time along randomised trajectories). Six steady-state outputs were tracked: HR, mean arterial pressure (MAP), delivered tidal volume, peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO$_2$), and arterial oxygen and carbon dioxide partial pressures (PaO$_2$, PaCO$_2$). Four clinical scenarios (a healthy baseline and three ARDS severities) crossed with two adult reference bodies, male and female, gave 1{,}280 simulations, collectively called the \emph{sweep}. A four-flag classifier labelled every run as refused at setup, diverged, physiologically implausible or not at steady state; flagged runs are categorised rather than discarded, so the atlas stays complete. We release this run-level classifier and a per-cell map that sets review priority. Neither is an external physiological validation, and the runs that pass screening are not a representative subset. The core sampling design, classifier thresholds and primary analysis plan were preregistered, deviations are reported explicitly, and the pipeline is reproducible from a single Docker image with a fixed seed (open-source, Apache-2.0).

\subsection{Overview of the reliability atlas}\label{sec:results-overview}

The atlas comprises 1{,}280 simulations in eight cells of 160 runs, one cell per clinical scenario (Healthy, Mild, Moderate and Severe ARDS; Table~\ref{tab:terms}) and sex (Figure~\ref{fig:design}). Every run carries the four preregistered flags (\texttt{clamped}, \texttt{diverged}, \texttt{implausible} and \texttt{not\_steady\_state}; Table~\ref{tab:flag_defs}), and Methods~\S\ref{sec:methods-detector} lists the hard physiological bounds behind \texttt{implausible}.

Three of the four preregistered hypotheses (H1--H4, Methods~\S\ref{sec:methods-stats}) are resolved by the flag counts (Table~\ref{tab:flag_summary}). H3 and H4 are supported: solver divergence occurs outside the previously characterised ARDS instability band (20 / 1{,}280 runs) and only on the male body (\S\ref{sec:results-sex}), an asymmetry that alone satisfies H4's criterion of a sex difference in clamping, divergence or $\mu^{*}$ ranking. H2 (silent input clamping) is \emph{not supported}: the \texttt{clamped} flag never fired (0 / 1{,}280), and because this log-based flag cannot detect silent substitution, a post hoc input-instantiation audit provided the complementary check. H1, which concerns input importance rather than any flag, is addressed in \S\ref{sec:results-morris}.

The audit compared every submitted value with the value Pulse serialises after ingestion and found exact agreement for all 10{,}160 input contracts (one value submitted in one configuration block) that reached ingestion. The remaining 80 of the 10{,}240 contracts are the ventilator contracts of the 20 runs that failed during patient stabilisation, before the ventilator action was submitted, and were excluded. Both results hold only within the sampled envelope. Pulse makes one explicit downstream adjustment: where FiO$_2$~=~1.0 was requested and the ventilator action was reached (195 runs), the ventilator model caps the inspired oxygen fraction at 0.9996 and logs an error, because the ambient mixture already carries CO$_2$ (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-clamp}).

Retention, the number of runs kept after screening, depends strongly on both the cell and the screen (Table~\ref{tab:flag_summary}). Under the four-flag screen, 663 of 1{,}280 runs (51.8~\%) are clean, with a 3.43-fold range across cells, from 144 of 160 in Moderate-M to 42 of 160 in Healthy-F. The hard-failure-free screen, which also admits runs flagged only \texttt{not\_steady\_state}, keeps 1{,}134 of 1{,}280 (88.6~\%), a 1.51-fold range. The two screens deplete opposite ends of the severity axis; Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s4} decomposes the \texttt{not\_steady\_state} runs that separate them. In the Healthy cells, the low four-flag retention mostly reflects the tidal-volume oscillation of \S\ref{sec:results-vcac} and SpO$_2$ pinned at saturation (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s4}). Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_light} gives the resulting curation map: one colour per (scenario, sex, output) combination, set by the cell's \texttt{diverged}-plus-\texttt{implausible} rate and by the deviation of the combination's clean-run mean from Healthy-M (Methods~\S\ref{sec:methods-traffic}).

\subsection{Primary input-sensitivity rankings}\label{sec:results-morris}

Figure~\ref{fig:morris} shows the Morris $\mu^{*}$ (input importance) of the seven swept inputs for each output and cell. H1, which predicts that the three most influential inputs carry at least half of each output's summed $\mu^{*}$, holds in all 48 cell $\times$ output combinations (range 0.658--0.998; median 0.928). The lowest fractions (0.658--0.727) all occur for gas-exchange outputs in Severe, where sensitivity spreads across more inputs. Cardiovascular outputs are driven by cardiovascular inputs (output HR by input HR, and MAP by SBP, in every cell; the SBP dominance is not an artefact of the pulse-pressure clip, Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-clip-cost}), whereas gas-exchange outputs are ventilator-driven: FiO$_2$ dominates PaO$_2$ in Mild and Moderate. In Severe, its $\mu^{*}$ on PaO$_2$ falls sharply; it is still rank 1 in Severe-M, but on a single elementary-effect pair, and is not estimable in Severe-F under the four-flag screen. Ventilator settings also reach the cardiovascular outputs, but unevenly: their $\mu^{*}$ reaches 13.9~bpm on output HR (FiO$_2$ in Moderate-F) but stays at or below 2.1~mmHg on MAP wherever it is estimable (Figure~\ref{fig:morris}).

Influence is not proportionality. Of the 144 top-three inputs, 23 (spread over 17 of the 48 combinations) exceed the preregistered $\sigma / \mu^{*} \geq 0.6$ threshold, where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of an input's elementary effects, with a 95~\% bootstrap interval at or above it under each of five independent seeds. All seven inputs and all four scenarios are represented, so departures from a linear, additive response are common rather than localised. This ratio is the standard Morris proxy for non-linearity \emph{or} input--input interaction and does not separate the two. Delivered tidal volume is the most affected output (8 of the 23), but this map is bounded by run survival, because the delivered-Vt oscillation that dominates the \texttt{not\_steady\_state} flags (\S\ref{sec:results-vcac}) removes the affected runs from the analysis. In the Healthy scenario, the delivered-Vt coefficient of variation exceeds the 0.05 threshold on 139 of 320 runs and none of the 8 falls there, whereas in the Moderate scenario it does so on 36 of 320 runs and 3 of the 8 fall there. The regime in which delivered volume oscillates most is thus the one in which that oscillation is least measurable.

Run survival also limits the data behind each index. Across the 144 top-three entries, the median index is computed from 7 of the 20 possible elementary-effect pairs; 25 rest on three or fewer pairs and 9 on a single pair, five of them their combination's rank-1 input. For these nine, all from tidal volume in Healthy-F and FiO$_2$ in Severe-M, $\sigma$ is zero by construction and no bootstrap interval is estimable. Per-entry pair counts are released with the indices.

The rankings were tested in two ways. The top-three input sets were cross-checked against a SHAP surrogate fitted on the same runs (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s13}) and re-derived under the hard-failure-free screen (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s13-screen}); H1 holds in all 48 combinations under both screens. In the 30 combinations whose candidate set (the inputs with at least one retained pair) is the same under both screens, the rank-1 input is preserved in all 30, whereas ranks two and three change in 11. Under this screen comparison, rank 1 was therefore more stable than the ordering below it, and the full ordering is not resolved by this design.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{VC-AC tidal-volume oscillation and the $-2$~cmH$_2$O trigger lever}{VC-AC tidal-volume oscillation and the -2 cmH2O trigger lever}}\label{sec:results-vcac}

The largest single source of \texttt{not\_steady\_state} flags is tidal-volume (Vt) oscillation under volume-controlled assist-control (VC-AC) ventilation, measured as the coefficient of variation (CV) of delivered Vt over the final 60~s of the 120~s recording. It is the \emph{primary} flag on 20.6~\% of runs: delivered volume keeps changing from breath to breath, a genuine oscillation rather than a solver failure. The effect concentrates in healthy lungs, is strongest at high set respiratory rate and normal compliance, and falls markedly with increasing ARDS severity. This pattern is consistent with \emph{patient--ventilator dyssynchrony}, well documented clinically in volume-control ventilation~\cite{figueroacasas2016}; the conditions that produce it here are an in-silico finding of this study.

A follow-up experiment isolated the mechanism. In 64 affected configurations, setting Pulse's \texttt{InspirationPatientTriggerPressure} (the airway-pressure drop below PEEP at which a patient effort triggers a breath) to $-2$~cmH$_2$O reduced the median Vt CV from 0.351 to 0.016, and 78.1~\% of reruns fell below the 0.05 steady-state threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:corrections}a). This lock is a data-generation lever, not a clinical recommendation. By default, the assisted breaths of VC-AC are triggered by the inspiratory efforts of the engine's respiratory model. The lock replaces this respiratory-model trigger with a fixed pressure threshold, loosening the coupling between breath delivery and the modelled respiratory drive that produces effort-dependent breath-to-breath variability in the default configuration. In a real patient, the threshold is a clinical choice: with any fixed threshold, this one included, efforts too weak to reach it fail to trigger a breath (ineffective triggering), a form of patient--ventilator dyssynchrony~\cite{thille2006} that is clinically undesirable~\cite{blanch2015}. Further detail, including the two most affected configurations: Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s8}.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Transient hyperoxia regime at FiO$_2$~=~1.0}{Transient hyperoxia regime at FiO2 = 1.0}}\label{sec:results-hyperoxia}

A second cluster of flags concentrates at the highest inspired-oxygen settings. Within the 120-second window, steady-state PaO$_2$ exceeds the 600~mmHg upper bound on 34 of the 126 \texttt{implausible} runs, 91~\% of them at FiO$_2 \geq 0.95$. To separate an engine defect from a window-length artefact, 60 configurations were re-run from time zero for 300~s, with the plateau taken over the final 60~s ($t = 240$--$300$~s). The result supports the window-length artefact: in the Healthy scenario, 40 of 44 reruns (90.9~\%) converged to a stable plateau under the preregistered drift criterion (Figure~\ref{fig:corrections}b), with a median of 643~mmHg over all 40, approximately 20~mmHg \emph{below} the alveolar-gas-equation prediction~\cite{west2020respiratory}. This rules out a cohort-wide oxygenation runaway, although validating the plateau level itself would require measured arterial data. Three of the 40 converged runs nevertheless plateau above 1{,}400~mmHg, which is physically impossible at sea level (760~mmHg); they belong to a six-run solver-instability regime (below), so 37 of 44 reruns (84.1~\%) converge below the wider 700~mmHg bound used in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s9}.

Hyperoxia studies on Pulse should therefore record at least 300 seconds before extracting a steady-state plateau. In the audited hyperoxia and Vt-oscillation runs, window-length sensitivity was concentrated in PaO$_2$ and delivered Vt, and threshold exceedances were uncommon for HR, MAP and PaCO$_2$ (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s16-window}). Because extended runs on healthy lungs at FiO$_2$ = 1.0 can also enter a separate solver-instability regime, far more often on the female than on the male body, each PaO$_2$ trajectory should be screened individually (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s9}).

\subsection{Sex-stratified solver divergence and blood-volume dependence}\label{sec:results-sex}

Solver divergence is rare but strikingly sex-stratified: \emph{all 20 diverged runs (1.6~\%) were on the male reference body}, and none of the 640 runs on the female body diverged (Figure~\ref{fig:sex_bv}a). The 20 events span only 8 distinct input vectors. FiO$_2$ was requested at its 1.0 boundary in 13 of them, although every divergence preceded the ventilator action that would have applied it (\S\ref{sec:results-overview}). The asymmetry is specific to these input extremes: at midrange inputs across the instability band (\S\ref{sec:results-secondary}), both bodies diverge, the female body mainly in the lower part of the band and the male body in its upper part (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s5-regimes}).

A follow-up experiment identified a blood-volume dependence. Pulse's patient-setup code (\texttt{SetupPatient.cpp}) computes baseline blood volume from body weight alone, with the same allometric formula for both sexes: 4{,}803~mL for the 67.3~kg male body and 3{,}996~mL for the 56.2~kg female body. Both bodies thus have about the same blood volume per kilogram ($\approx$71~mL\,kg$^{-1}$, within standard clinical estimates~\cite{nadler1962}) and differ only in absolute volume. We re-ran the 20 diverged male configurations with the baseline imposed through Pulse's \texttt{BloodVolumeBaseline} patient setting. Divergences fell from 20 / 20 at the default to 0 / 20 both at an intermediate 4{,}400~mL and at the engine's setup floor for this body, 4{,}082~mL (Figure~\ref{fig:sex_bv}b). Removing divergence does not by itself make a configuration usable: 12 of the 20 are clean at both levels, whereas 8 keep a flag already defined by the classifier (SpO$_2$ pinned at saturation, the tidal-volume oscillation of \S\ref{sec:results-vcac}, or the high-FiO$_2$ PaO$_2$ modes of \S\ref{sec:results-hyperoxia}; Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s10}).

Steady-state values separate the two bodies far less than divergence does. Across 24 (scenario $\times$ output) comparisons, the Wasserstein distance between male and female clean-run distributions exceeds its permutation null in six, five of which survive Benjamini--Hochberg correction (Methods~\S\ref{sec:methods-stats}), all for gas exchange (PaCO$_2$ in Healthy, Mild and Moderate; SpO$_2$ and PaO$_2$ in Severe). Heart rate, MAP and delivered tidal volume separate no further than under random relabelling of the pooled runs. Input-importance rankings move even less: in Mild and Moderate, the only scenarios where the comparison is computable, male and female Morris rankings are concordant (Spearman $\rho$ point estimates 0.75--1.00 across 12 combinations), and only delivered tidal volume in Mild has a confidence interval that includes zero (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s6-rho}). Between the two bodies, where the solver fails thus differs far more than which inputs dominate.

These cross-body contrasts call for caution. They are computed on clean runs, whose number itself differs by sex (e.g.\ 144 vs 122 of 160 in Moderate), so a surviving distance cannot separate an effect on the physiology from an effect on which configurations pass the flag filter. Gas-exchange contrasts also include a systematic ventilation-dose difference: at the same absolute tidal volume (the sweep sets Vt in millilitres, not per kilogram, and identically for both bodies), the female body receives 19.8~\% more per kilogram of actual body weight and 36.1~\% more per kilogram of ideal body weight (sex-specific Devine relation applied by Pulse at setup; Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s6-dose}). Moreover, Pulse's sex flag simultaneously changes body composition, basal metabolic rate, haemoglobin, cardiovascular sizing and, through ideal body weight, anatomic dead space (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s10-breadth}), so no cross-body distance can be attributed to a single physiological pathway. None of this affects the divergence finding, which rests on a direct intervention on absolute blood volume rather than on a cross-body contrast.

\subsection{Secondary observations}\label{sec:results-secondary}

Four further observations bear on interpretation (Supplementary Information, indexed in \S\,\ref{sec:s17-summary}): Pulse's ARDS impairment-fraction solver-instability band; a compensatory tachycardia that lifts mean output heart rate above the sampled input range in moderate and severe ARDS, although input heart rate remains its dominant within-scenario determinant; a preregistered Sobol analysis (which apportions output variance among inputs and their interactions) that was triggered but not executed because the triggered design exceeded its locked compute budget; and a limitation of the flatline rule itself.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

This work delivers the first open reliability atlas of the Pulse Physiology Engine across several adult critical-care scenarios, with a preregistered core design and primary analysis plan. The 1{,}280-run sweep yields two operator-actionable corrections and one sex-stratified failure mode. First, a single configuration setting markedly stabilises VC-AC tidal-volume delivery for synthetic-cohort generation, at the cost of suppressing much of the effort-dependent variability that Pulse models; it is a data-generation tool, not a clinical recommendation (\S\ref{sec:results-vcac}). Second, hyperoxia simulations at FiO$_2 = 1.0$ should be recorded for at least 300~s, rather than the 120~s used here, before a steady-state plateau is taken (\S\ref{sec:results-hyperoxia}). Third, a previously uncharacterised solver-divergence cluster (runs that end without usable output) is confined to the male body in an input-extreme regime and depends on blood volume (\S\ref{sec:results-sex}).

The per-cell traffic-light map (Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_light}) is the principal deliverable for downstream users. Its yellow--red boundary deviates materially from the preregistered wording; this post hoc change is declared with a sensitivity analysis in \S\ref{sec:methods-traffic}. The map is a curation and review-priority instrument, not a physiological validation of the engine. Credibility frameworks in biomedical modelling treat sensitivity analysis and a bounded domain of applicability as components of assessment, but validation requires comparison against measured data~\cite{viceconti2021insilico}, which this work does not perform. Of a proposed five-stage validation scheme for synthetic biomedical data, running from internal plausibility through downstream utility, out-of-distribution generalisation and simulation-to-reality transfer to biological or clinical coupling~\cite{victoriano2026sim2real}, the atlas addresses only part of the first stage: it tests whether runs are internally plausible, not whether they match real-world distributions.

The four-flag classifier can also screen Pulse-generated synthetic cohorts before they are used to train machine-learning models, a role comparable to the verification and filtering stages of the synthetic-data pipeline cited in the Introduction, where each synthesised agent task (one the model performs by acting in a software environment) carries its own verification system~\cite{deepseek2026v41flash}. Unlike those stages, the classifier checks how the simulator behaved, not whether its outputs agree with patient data. Because its \texttt{clamped} flag sees only the rejections that Pulse logs, this screening relies on the null result of \S\ref{sec:results-overview}: within the sampled envelope, no submitted input was silently altered at ingestion. This concerns setup-time input handling only; it says nothing about how physiological states evolve during a run or about Pulse's behaviour outside the sampled envelope. The map sets cell-level review priority, but run-level screening remains necessary whatever the cell colour, because a tile's colour does not count \texttt{not\_steady\_state} runs, and every use remains subject to use-case-specific validation. The green set is the same under both non-degenerate readings of the rule, and green does not establish physiological validity. The map also serves educators: Pulse is embedded in training products that present healthcare learners with real-time physiological and pharmacological data~\cite{pulseinaction}, and instructors can use it to avoid regimes in which the engine diverges or never settles.

Four findings call for care when Pulse-generated data are reused. First, pooling male and female runs from the input-extreme regime without flag-based filtering introduces a spurious sex covariate through the divergence cluster (\S\ref{sec:results-sex}). Second, input and output heart rates are not interchangeable in ARDS scenarios because of compensatory tachycardia (\S\ref{sec:results-secondary}), even though input heart rate still drives output heart rate within each scenario; a model trained on such cohorts would otherwise learn an active response to hypoxemia as a steady-state feature. Third, sex-conditioned distributional differences persist even among clean runs (\S\ref{sec:results-sex}); they may be physiological, but they have no single mechanism and no scenario-independent direction of effect, so they cannot be read as validated sex effects. Fourth, the four-flag screen excludes every run showing the tidal-volume oscillation of \S\ref{sec:results-vcac}, most often in healthy lungs, so a cohort filtered that way under-represents it (the hard-failure-free screen admits runs flagged only \texttt{not\_steady\_state}; \S\ref{sec:results-overview}).

For the computational-physiology community, the atlas directly addresses the maintainers' statement that no formal sensitivity or uncertainty quantification has been performed on user-facing operational inputs~\cite{pulsefaq}, and shows that sensitivity is concentrated in a few inputs in every cell $\times$ output combination tested (\S\ref{sec:results-morris}). The method also carries over to other engines: a Morris screen of the operational inputs paired with a classifier that sorts every run into a category instead of discarding failures. To our knowledge, no comparable public reliability atlas combining Morris screening with explicit clamping analysis has been published for BioGears~\cite{baird2020biogears} or HumMod~\cite{hester2011hummod}; the same recipe could be applied to them by wrapping each engine's own inputs and outputs.

Prior falsification work on MIMIC-III is complementary rather than overlapping. Of the 85 Hoeffding-inequality rejections reported by Cornish et al.~\cite{cornish2023}, 80 fall on channels the atlas does not assess, 2 on the partially overlapping pressure channel SysBP, and only 3 on the four channels assessed by both studies (HR, MAP, PaO$_2$, PaCO$_2$; Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s7}). Cornish et al.\ test Pulse's chemistry and dermal outputs against a real cohort undergoing clinical interventions, whereas the atlas tests its cardio-respiratory response to operational inputs. Neither maps how operational inputs drive the electrolyte channels most often flagged by Cornish et al., so a metabolic-and-renal reliability atlas is the natural next step.

For the engine's maintainers, the atlas provides, to our knowledge, the first systematic characterisation of an ARDS impairment-fraction solver-instability band ($[0.879, 0.946]$), together with engine-level observations that target tooling and validation infrastructure rather than Pulse's underlying physiology (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s11}).

Six scope limits bound the conclusions (details in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s2-scope}). (i)~The atlas is pinned to Pulse v4.3.1; results apply identically to the build-only v4.3.2 and may not carry over unchanged to v4.4.0, released after the sweep with revised physiology models. (ii)~Only two young, lean adult reference bodies were used (age 18, the youngest age Pulse accepts; body-mass index about 21), so variation in age, and in mass and height within each sex, is not characterised. (iii)~Only adult VC-AC ventilation was covered; pressure-targeted modes and within-run transients are out of scope by design. (iv)~The Morris estimator discards any pair containing a flagged run, which removes every FiO$_2$ pair in Healthy-M, Healthy-F and Severe-F; these FiO$_2$ entries are blank in Figure~\ref{fig:morris}, and the cross-sex Spearman comparison is confined to Mild and Moderate. (v)~The hard physiological bounds judge whether a \emph{sustained} steady-state mean is plausible, not whether a value is instantaneously attainable; they are a screening standard, not a claim that exceptional clinical trajectories are impossible. (vi)~Pulse enforces DBP/SBP $\leq$ 0.75, and the resulting clip shortened 9 of 20 DBP steps, so some SBP and DBP effects are not strict fixed-step Morris estimates. In addition, no trajectory-count convergence analysis was performed, and shunt fraction was not recorded, so shunt-mediated interpretations remain physiologically motivated rather than directly tested.

The atlas is intended as a baseline for further work. The classifier can be applied to new runs recorded with the same protocol, and the traffic-light map to configurations within its eight cells, without re-running the sweep. Validating the cell-level classifications against clinical databases such as MIMIC-IV would test them against real patient care; it would require replaying recorded patient states and ventilator settings in the engine and comparing the result with the next observed state. Simulated and real-world cohorts are complementary rather than interchangeable: mechanistic simulation provides controlled inputs and directly accessible model states, whereas real-world data retain the messiness of clinical practice. Whether a model trained on simulated cohorts performs on real patients is a further question. One sim-to-real study quantified the distributional mismatch between its simulated training data and real-world datasets and evaluated its model on the latter~\cite{hwang2025sim2real}; the atlas does neither. Models trained only on simulated data and tested on real patients, for ventilator-asynchrony detection and aortic pressure estimation, performed reasonably, though below models trained on real data~\cite{bakkes2023asynchrony,bikia2020aortic}.

Pulse-generated cohorts are already used as machine-learning training data~\cite{ward2024ventilator}, and ventilation automation is reaching the bedside: in a recent multicentre randomised trial, closed-loop ventilation improved overall ventilation quality yet showed no difference in patient-centred outcomes~\cite{sinnige2026active}. Characterising a simulator's operational limits is therefore a precondition for using it to train models that may inform such care. The longer-term aim is models that adapt to each patient's own responses, as clinicians do at the bedside, rather than applying cohort averages.

\section{Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Pulse engine and configuration}\label{sec:methods-pulse}

All simulations used Pulse v4.3.1 (Kitware, Apache-2.0)~\cite{bray2019}, a real-time-capable lumped-parameter whole-body physiology engine whose respiratory sub-model is a 5-compartment lung~\cite{pulserespmethod}. The engine ran inside a single \texttt{pulse-atlas-engine} Docker image, pinned for the 1{,}280-run sweep so that every simulation runs against bit-identical engine binaries. A thin Python layer around Pulse's command-line interface wrote one JSON configuration per Morris input vector (reference body, ARDS scenario, ventilator actions and the seven sampled inputs) and launched one container per run.

Re-running the pipeline reproduces the canonical outputs bit-for-bit at three successive stages, each checked against a committed artefact: the input sample, the per-cell aggregate tables and the derived statistics. Mechanical ventilation was restricted to volume-controlled assist-control (VC-AC) mode so that all runs remain directly comparable; the sweep runs used Pulse's default patient trigger. Hardware and environment details are given in Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s12-env}.

\subsection{Patient reference bodies}\label{sec:methods-patients}

Two adult reference bodies were used: male (age 18, 67.3~kg, 180~cm) and female (age 18, 56.2~kg, 163~cm). Both weights are the U.S.\ Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) growth-chart 50th-percentile weight-for-age values for each sex~\cite{kuczmarski2002cdc} (\texttt{Agemos}~=~216.5). The female height matches the CDC 50th percentile and Pulse's standard female height; the male height was fixed by the researchers (full provenance: Supplement~\S\ref{sec:s3-bodies}).

\subsection{Sweep design}\label{sec:methods-sweep}

Seven user-facing operational inputs were swept across the following ranges: HR 50--110~bpm, SBP 90--120~mmHg, DBP 60--80~mmHg (subject to the pulse-pressure clip below), RR 8--20~breaths\,min$^{-1}$, Vt 300--800~mL, FiO$_2$ 0.21--1.0 and PEEP 0--20~cmH$_2$O. The HR, blood-pressure and RR ranges are Pulse's own setup acceptance bands, whereas the Vt, FiO$_2$ and PEEP ranges are clinical ranges fixed in the preregistration. MAP was derived as $\mathrm{DBP} + (\mathrm{SBP} - \mathrm{DBP})/3$ rather than swept independently. The swept heart-rate and blood-pressure values are setup values honoured at ingestion, not setpoints maintained against the engine's own reflexes: Pulse re-baselines them to the stabilised state at the end of stabilisation, and no action used here rewrites them.

Pulse refuses some setup values outright, and the sampler ranges were set inside the two constraints that act on swept inputs: a gate on systolic and diastolic blood pressure, and acceptance bands on the initial heart and respiratory rates (both verified against the Pulse source and by boundary-probe runs before the sweep; Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s2}). A third constraint, Pulse's limit on the pulse-pressure ratio, was deliberately not imposed on the Morris sample, because doing so would distort the sample geometry on which the elementary-effect estimator depends. It was applied instead when each configuration was written (a sampled DBP above 0.75~$\times$~SBP was lowered to 0.75~$\times$~SBP), and every run records the requested value, the applied value and a clip flag (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-clip-cost}). Every configuration submitted to Pulse therefore lies inside the accepted envelope. A fourth constraint, a per-patient floor on baseline blood volume, is not exercised by the sweep and is characterised for the male body in \S\ref{sec:results-sex}.

Four scenarios spanned the adult critical-care severity range: a healthy baseline (Healthy, ARDS impairment fraction = 0) and three ARDS severities (Mild, 0.26; Moderate, 0.60; Severe, 0.95). At a low-tidal-volume reference ventilation, these impairment fractions place each scenario in a Berlin P/F band for the male reference body, anchoring them to a clinically recognisable state rather than to arbitrary engine settings (calibration and its sweep-wide FiO$_2$ dependence: Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s5-berlin}). The mild, moderate and severe labels name the band each scenario was calibrated to, not a diagnosis of each run, since individual runs vary FiO$_2$ and PEEP. The three severities also bracket the 0.91--0.945 solver-instability zone documented at design time (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s5}), which our continuous-severity sweep later widened to $[0.879, 0.946]$ (\S\ref{sec:results-secondary}). Pulse represents severity as a single per-lung scalar driving four hard-coded, adult-calibrated interpolation curves (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s5-mapping}).

Sensitivity was screened with the Morris elementary-effects design~\cite{morris1991, campolongo2007}, whose cost is linear in the number of inputs, so that the same runs both map where the engine fails and rank which inputs drive each output. The sample was generated with SALib~\cite{iwanaga2022,herman2017salib} using 20 trajectories, 4 grid levels per input and random seed 42. Each trajectory contributes $7 + 1 = 8$ model evaluations, giving 160 runs per (scenario $\times$ sex) cell and 1{,}280 runs in total. The trajectory count was fixed at 20 in the preregistration for computational tractability, not through a convergence analysis. Each run was recorded for 120 seconds of simulated time after patient stabilisation and a 120-second ventilator-settling phase, with outputs sampled every 5 seconds (13 samples in the final 60-second steady-state window).

Follow-up runs used fixed rules. The trigger-lock reruns (\S\ref{sec:results-vcac}) took the 64 non-diverged sweep runs with the highest delivered-Vt CV. The 300-second reruns (\S\ref{sec:results-hyperoxia}) took the 22 Morris points requested at FiO$_2$ = 1.0 that diverged in no cell, in both Healthy cells, and the first four, in sample order, in the Moderate and Severe cells. The continuous-severity sweep cited in \S\ref{sec:results-sex} ran 100 impairment fractions, drawn at random and more densely above 0.85, on both bodies, with the seven inputs at the midpoints of their swept ranges (FiO$_2$ rounded to 0.6). Details: Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s8-protocol}, \S\,\ref{sec:s9-trajectories} and \S\,\ref{sec:s5-cont}.

\subsection{Steady-state classifier}\label{sec:methods-detector}

Every simulation was classified by a four-flag steady-state classifier before any statistical analysis.

\texttt{clamped} fires when Pulse's setup log contains one of the messages Pulse writes when it rejects a setup input (the name comes from preregistered hypothesis H2, which predicted silent clamping). This log-based detector identifies \emph{explicit} setup rejection only; silent substitution was checked by the post hoc input-instantiation audit reported in \S\ref{sec:results-overview}. The sweep was classified with 4 message patterns; after the sweep, one pattern was widened and a fifth added to catch Pulse's heart-rate lower-bound rejection. Replaying both pattern sets over all 3{,}349 recorded setup logs reclassifies no run of this sweep (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-clamp}).

\texttt{diverged} fires when a run does not finish with finite output: a non-zero exit code, a missing or truncated CSV, or NaN or infinite values. Although a setup rejection would also exit non-zero, in this sweep each of the 20 diverged runs carries Pulse's own \texttt{Could not converge to provided Convergence} message in its setup log, and none of the 1{,}260 completed runs does (\texttt{results/divergence\_timeline.csv}).

\texttt{implausible} fires when any steady-state output mean falls outside the locked hard physiological bounds: HR [20, 220]~bpm, MAP [30, 180]~mmHg, Vt [50, 1{,}500]~mL, SpO$_2$ [50, 100]~\%, PaO$_2$ [20, 600]~mmHg, PaCO$_2$ [10, 120]~mmHg. Pulse imposes no acceptance envelope on these outputs; it does stop a run on some states far outside physiology (e.g.\ an aortic pressure below $-2$ or above 700~mmHg), but no run of this sweep reached one. The bounds are therefore our own post-run plausibility standard, not a constraint the engine enforces (rationale and literature sources: Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-hard-bounds}).

\texttt{not\_steady\_state} fires when, over the final 60-second window, any tracked output flatlines, drifts beyond its per-channel threshold (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-drift}) or has a coefficient of variation above 0.05. A flatline is a standard deviation below 1~\% of the mean \emph{and} a mean within 0.1~\% of a hard bound ($\geq$~99.9~\% for the SpO$_2$ ceiling). The rule was written to catch an output pinned numerically at a bound, but a physiologically saturated SpO$_2$ meets it too (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s4}).

The four flags are independent, so one run can fire several. Analyses that need one category per run keep the highest-ranked flag, in an order set after the sweep: \texttt{clamped} $>$ \texttt{diverged} $>$ \texttt{implausible} $>$ \texttt{not\_steady\_state} $>$ \texttt{clean}, which follows where a run fails (at setup, during the run, then in its output; Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-detector-order}). The [60, 120]~s window was preregistered before any sweep data were collected, and a 22-run pilot on the Healthy-M cell verified five preregistered gating criteria, including detector self-consistency and Pulse determinism.

\subsection{Traffic-light curation map}\label{sec:methods-traffic}

The curation map (Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_light}) gives each (scenario, sex, output) combination one colour (green, yellow or red) that combines the cell's \texttt{diverged}-plus-\texttt{implausible} rate with the deviation of the combination's clean-run mean from the corresponding clean-run mean of the Healthy-M cell. The $10~\%$ and $30~\%$ deviation bands were preregistered before any data were collected. The legend to Figure~\ref{fig:traffic_light} gives the full colour criteria; scoring the female cells against Healthy-F instead changes no colour (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-traffic}).

\textbf{Post hoc deviation from the preregistered traffic-light rule.} The preregistered rule gave red when the deviation exceeded $30~\%$ or when ``any flag'' was raised. The implemented rule counts only the hard flags, \texttt{diverged} and \texttt{implausible}: they make a combination at least yellow, and red only when their combined rate exceeds $30~\%$. Because the repository cannot establish whether this rule was defined before or after the sweep outputs were inspected, we record the altered yellow--red boundary as a material deviation. Read literally, with ``flag'' taken as the two hard flags, the preregistered rule keeps the same seven green and twelve red combinations and turns 26 of the 29 yellow ones red. We retain the implemented rule as the primary display because it preserves a graded distinction without adding a single green combination. The classifier, the map and the reported counts were not changed in response; Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-tl-sensitivity} reports the preregistered readings, including the four-flag one, as a sensitivity analysis.

\subsection{Statistical analyses}\label{sec:methods-stats}

Morris $\mu^{*}$ and $\sigma$ were computed per (scenario, sex, input, output) using only elementary-effect pairs whose two runs are both clean. Each elementary effect divides the output change by the input step, with each input's swept range mapped to $[0,1]$, so $\mu^{*}$ is an output change per full-range move of the input. Indices resting on at least two trajectories carry non-parametric 95~\% bootstrap confidence intervals from 1{,}000 trajectory-level resamples. Four hypotheses were preregistered before data collection. H1: the top-three inputs account for at least half of the summed $\mu^{*}$ for each output. H2: at least 5~\% of the Morris sample hits at least one silent clamp. H3: at least one solver-divergence zone exists outside the previously characterised 0.91--0.945 band within the seven-input adult envelope. H4: the male and female reference bodies differ in clamping, divergence or $\mu^{*}$ ranking on at least one output, with no direction prespecified. H1 was evaluated per cell $\times$ output on point estimates of $\mu^{*}$; its summed $\mu^{*}$ covers only inputs with at least one clean pair (six rather than seven in the 18 combinations where FiO$_2$ has none; Figure~\ref{fig:morris}), so the denominators differ across cells. H2, H3 and H4 are resolved in \S\ref{sec:results-overview}.

Sex differences in steady-state distributions were measured per (scenario $\times$ output) by the Wasserstein distance (the minimum ``work'' needed to reshape one probability distribution into the other) between the male and female clean-run distributions. Each distance was tested against a permutation null of 2{,}000 random relabellings at the original group sizes, and the 24 tests were corrected by Benjamini--Hochberg control of the false-discovery rate at $q = 0.05$. Cross-sex agreement of input-importance rankings was quantified per (scenario, output) by the Spearman rank correlation between the male and female $\mu^{*}$ vectors.

A Sobol analysis was preregistered as a conditional escalation for any top-three input whose $\sigma / \mu^{*}$ ratio reaches 0.6, within a compute envelope of 1{,}500 additional runs. Applied as preregistered, the trigger fires on 72 of the 144 top-three inputs (43 of the 48 combinations), and the design it selects needs 6{,}144 runs (3{,}584 for the first-order and total indices only), 2.4 to 4.1 times that envelope; it was therefore reported rather than executed (Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-sobol}). Because the preregistered trigger rests on the point ratio alone, we added a post hoc sensitivity analysis: for each top-three input, trajectories were resampled 1{,}000 times and $\sigma / \mu^{*}$ was recomputed on each draw. Inputs whose 95~\% interval has a lower bound at or above 0.6 under each of five independent seeds are reported in \S\ref{sec:results-morris} (full decomposition: Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s3-sobol}).

The sampler, sweep driver, classifier, statistical-analysis pipeline and audit ledger are released open-source. The audit ledger, developed after data collection, recomputes the paper's computed claims from the saved output files and checks them against the text.

\subsection{Use of AI tools}\label{sec:methods-ai}

\noindent Large language models (LLMs) were used in this work, under direct supervision by S.N., for drafting, editing and shortening the manuscript, for writing, refactoring and debugging the code that runs and analyses the sweep, and for in-repository replications and primary-source audits. During iterative review, two LLMs worked in separate sessions: always a Claude model (Anthropic; Opus 4.7, 4.8, 5 and 5.5 in succession), and Google Gemini Pro 3.1, later OpenAI ChatGPT (GPT-5.6 Sol). The Claude model in particular was often asked to re-check its own output, claim by claim and number by number, and to correct it; the two models then critiqued each other's latest output in turn. These checks often surfaced errors or missed points, which were then corrected. S.N.\ used the critiques to decide whether to run another round or accept the result. M.S.\ used a Claude model (Anthropic) to propose editorial revisions during his review; each proposal was checked against the data and sources before S.N.\ accepted or rejected it. Supplement~\S\,\ref{sec:s18} gives each model's exact version, reasoning level, phases and tasks.

Responsibility rests with all the authors. S.N.\ personally reviewed and, as far as practicable, verified AI-assisted outputs, including editorial revisions, consistency checks and methodological interpretations, and reviewed and committed AI-assisted code changes. Verification was an iterative process of checks. S.N.\ authored and locked the preregistered thresholds, hypotheses, statistical-test choices and experimental decisions before the corresponding analyses were run. Every cited reference was checked against its source. Although the atlas code was written with LLM assistance, the simulations, the classifier and the audit ledger run without any LLM. Quantitative claims are traceable, as applicable, to primary sources, the preregistration, or entries in the open audit ledger released alongside the code, whose commit history records the audit findings, their evidence and the resulting corrections.
